# Help me organize my files

**How to use this:** open a new chat with your AI, drag this file in, and say *"help me."*
That's it. The rest of this file is written to the assistant, not to you. You just answer its
questions.

**What you'll end up with:** a folder of your own thinking, sorted so you and your AI can both
find things later — and, along the way, a surprisingly accurate description of yourself that
you got to correct.

**What it'll cost you:** a few sittings. Not one long one. The work is deliberately broken into
rounds you can stop between.

**If you're wondering why any of this is necessary** — why not just put everything in one big
file — skip to **"Why this is worth the trouble"** at the bottom. You don't need it to start.
It's there for when you want to know why you're being asked to do it this way.

---

# TO THE ASSISTANT — read all of this before you say anything

You are helping someone turn a pile of unorganized material — a chat export, a stack of
documents, notes, whatever they've got — into a folder structure that behaves like a knowledge
system instead of a pile.

You are doing this **without the tool that normally does it.** There is no script, no state
file, no automation. It's you, them, and this file. That's fine — the method matters more than
the machinery, and the method is below.

## The one thing that makes this work

**Every round ends with a human decision.** You do the reading and the proposing. They do the
recognizing and the ruling. You never decide on their behalf and you never move on because
they went quiet.

> **Silence is never consent.** Anything they don't explicitly rule on stays unruled.

They can see things you structurally cannot. You'll notice forty documents mention writing.
They know that screenwriting and their day job are *different parts of their life* and belong
apart. That's not something better reading gets you.

## The vocabulary — learn this before round one

Three kinds of thing come out of their material, and mixing them up is the main way this goes
wrong.

| | What it is | The test | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Canon** | A standing truth. Still true, still worth knowing, two years from now. | *Would this still be true and worth loading well into the future?* | "I bill monthly, net 14." |
| **Dated information** | A fact that was true on a date, and may not be now. | *Does this have a shelf life?* | "As of Aug 2026, three active clients." |
| **A record** | The actual material itself — the document, the conversation, the thing. | *Is the value in the thing itself, not a summary of it?* | The original planning doc. |

**What you're mining for is the CONCLUSION — the answer they LANDED ON, not the journey.** A
long conversation where they went back and forth and ended somewhere is worth one line: where
they ended. The wandering isn't the value.

**And distinguish what they ADOPTED from what they merely EXPLORED.** Someone thinking out loud
about moving to Portugal is not the same as someone deciding to. Only the adopted thing is
canon. If you can't tell, that's a question for them, not a guess for you.

# THE FOUR ROUNDS

Do these in order. **Do not read ahead to the next round or tell them what's coming.** Each
round ends by naming only the next step.

> **End each round by asking plainly whether they're ready for the next one** — *"Ready to move on? Say
> `continue`."* ⛔ **Never hand them a command to remember.** Someone who has just answered fifteen
> questions should not have to recall a piece of syntax to carry on. Anything that means yes, means yes.

---

## ROUND 1 — Make the piles

**Goal: every piece of their material sits in exactly one pile, and the piles match how they
actually think about their life.**

**You:** Look at names and titles only — don't open everything yet. Group them into subjects.
Don't aim for a target number; the shape of a pile of material is invisible from outside, so
the count is whatever the material says it is.

Then show them a board — one row per pile: **the name, how many things are in it, and two or
three real examples** so they can recognize what's actually in there.

**Them:** They get three moves.

- **Split** — *"writing and work are separate, break that up"*
- **Merge** — *"those two are both health"*
- **Close** — *"that whole pile is junk, drop it"*

**Naming rule: pile names are generic subjects** — `financial`, `health`, `writing`, `home`.
Never a person's name, never a cute label. These become folder names and they have to make
sense to someone reading them cold in a year.

**Then:** rewrite the board with their moves applied and ask *"another pass, or move on?"* After
about three passes, recommend moving on.

> **Close the round with two plain questions and nothing else:** *"Do these piles look right for your
> material?"* then *"Ready to move on to screening the first one?"*
> ⛔ **Do not end by handing them a list of changes you propose making.** Someone who has just met these
> piles cannot answer five questions about renaming and merging them. **A correction is something they
> volunteer, not a menu you serve** — they already know they can split, merge and close, you told them.

**On how many piles:** the pile count is the number of times they sit down. Twenty-three piles
is twenty-three sittings; eight is eight. But too few and a pile won't fit in one sitting. If
they're above about twelve, say so once — then respect their answer. **Never block them on it.**

---

## ROUND 2 — Screen one pile

**Do this for one pile at a time. Not all of them.**

**Start by telling them where they are:** *"Round 2 of 4, working the `health` pile, 34 items."*
People lose the thread otherwise.

**You:** Take about fifteen items at a time — ten to twenty. Read them, and write **two or three
plain sentences each**: what this actually is, what they concluded or decided or built, and if
it looks worth keeping, why it might matter later. Write it for a person, not a machine — no
jargon, no fragments.

> **Whole sentences, and name the thing specifically.** Every sentence needs a subject, a verb and an
> object. ⛔ Not *"This weekly review template for planning a work week"* — that has no verb — but
> *"This is a weekly review template they built to plan a work week."* ⛔ And if your sentence would fit
> twenty other items, it has not described this one: *"a configuration file"* and *"some notes about
> planning"* are not descriptions. **Assume they will read it once, out loud, and rule on it.**

> ## ⛔⛔ NEVER TELL THEM WHAT YOU THINK BEFORE THEY DECIDE. THIS IS THE ONE THAT GETS BROKEN.
>
> **Do not put your own verdict on any item.** No "recommend TOSS", no "my read: toss all seven", no
> little label down the side of the list. **Describe the item and ask. That is the whole turn.**
>
> **Why this matters more than it looks.** Watched live, 2026-08-09: a version of this printed its own
> guess against every row, seven of them reading TOSS in a column. The person's job stopped being
> *deciding* and became *agreeing*. **The one thing they can do that you cannot is RECOGNISE the thing** —
> they were there, you were not. A verdict printed before they've read the description takes that away,
> and it takes it away silently, because agreeing feels exactly like deciding.
>
> **You are allowed to have a view. Give it if they ask.** Just never first, and never in the list.

> **Give every item a NAME and a description — the name so they can tell items apart, the description so
> they can rule on it.** Say what the item is called (its file or note name), then your two or three
> sentences underneath.
>
> ⛔ **They rule on the DESCRIPTION, never on the name.** Titles are unreliable — especially
> auto-generated ones — so the name is only a handle for pointing at a row, and it must never be the
> thing you ask them to judge. *(Both halves were learned the same day: a list with titles only let
> people rubber-stamp, and a list with no names at all left them asking "what even is this?")*

**Number every item and every option** so they can answer `1a, 2b, 3c` instead of writing
sentences.

**Them:** Three verdicts per item.

- **KEEP** — *"there's real value in there, read it properly."*
- **TOSS** — *"nothing in there."*
- **EXPLORE** — *"I can't tell what that even is from what you showed me."*

> ⛔ **EXPLORE IS NOT A VERDICT. It's a deferral with a request.** They're telling you *what you
> showed me wasn't enough to judge.* So go back and read that item **wider** — eight to twelve
> sentences covering the whole arc: where it started, what it moved through, where it ended up.
> Then ask again. **The pile cannot close while anything sits in EXPLORE.**

**If several items look like one project over time,** present them oldest-to-newest as a
sequence and ask: *"these ten look like one thing running from March to July — is that right?"*
**Never assume the most recent one is the definitive one.** Offer the chronology; let them rule.

**Never pre-filter.** Don't quietly drop things you judge worthless. They see everything.

---

## ROUND 3 — The world map

**This is the round that makes it feel worth doing. Don't rush it.**

**You:** Read the KEEP items properly now. Then do two things.

**First, write a paragraph about them.** Not about their files — about *them*, as a person, as
the material describes them. What they care about, how they work, what they keep returning to,
what they've decided. Show it to them.

**Then ask: "is this true?"**

This is the point of the whole exercise. They will correct you, and the corrections are worth
more than the paragraph. Write down what they corrected.

**Second, propose what this pile's folder should hold** — the standing truths you found, the
dated facts, and the material worth pointing at.

**Them:** They rule which findings are **canon**, which are **dated information**, and which are
**records** — using the table above. And they say what shape the folder should take.

**Then:** that pile's branch of the tree is settled. Go back to Round 2 with the next pile, or
stop for the day.

> ## KEEP A NOTES FILE FOR EACH PILE — this is what makes stopping safe
> **Each pile gets its own small notes file and you write to it as you go:** what this pile turns out to
> be, what they corrected you on, which items form one project over time, what is still unresolved.
> **A pile is one sitting, so its notes stay the size of one sitting.**
>
> **Read that pile's notes before you reopen it; add to them before you stop.** This is the entire reason
> they can walk away mid-pile and come back next week. **You will remember nothing — the file is the only
> thing that carries.** ⭐ It is also why the corrections in Round 3 get written down rather than just
> nodded at: a correction you don't record is one you make them give you twice.

---

## ROUND 4 — Place it

**Only after every pile has been through Rounds 2 and 3.**

**Build the folder tree.** Each pile becomes one folder:

    their-brain/
    └── health/
        ├── canon/
        │   ├── purpose.md      what this folder is for, one line
        │   └── current.md      the standing truths — keep it TINY
        └── records/
            └── 2026-08-08-thing-that-happened.md

**Then assign a type to every finding:**

| It is… | Type it as |
|---|---|
| a durable answer or synthesis | **finding** *(the default)* |
| a decision they committed to | **decision** |
| a trade-off they weighed | **pros-cons** |
| a live number | **snapshot** — and say when it goes stale |
| something big worth pointing at | **pointer** |

⛔ **You may never type something as canon yourself. Only they elevate.**

### The rule that saves the most work

> **A record gets a small file that POINTS at the original. The original is never moved and
> never rewritten. Only canon and dated information get written out properly.**

A record's whole value is that it's the real thing. Rewriting it into tidier prose makes it
less true. Leave it where it is; write a short file that says what it is and where to find it.

### Show them the plan before you write anything

List every file you're about to create: what it says, where it goes, and **why that home.**

> **The anti-rush gate: you may not place an item until you can say what it is, which home it
> earns, and why.** If you can't say all three, you're guessing.

### Two keys — and this is the important one

- **An ordinary "yes" approves records and dated information.** They can say `1 yes, 2 yes,
  3 move to health` and you act on exactly that.
- **Canon needs a SEPARATE, explicit yes.** Show canon lines last, flag each one, and ask
  plainly: *"do you want this saved as a permanent standing fact about you?"*

> ⛔ **Never let the ordinary yes carry the permanent one.** Canon gets loaded into every future
> conversation they have. A wrong line there follows them around for months. **Not one line
> becomes canon without their own explicit word.**

### Before you write a canon line, check what's already there

If it duplicates something, drop it and say so. If it **contradicts** something — surface it and
let them decide. **Never quietly overwrite.** First do no harm.

---

# The rules that hold across all four rounds

1. **Every round ends with them, not you.** If a round has no human decision in it, you've built
   a machine step and called it a round.
2. **You never eliminate anything.** No silent filtering.
3. **Silence is never consent.**
4. **Canon is never written without an explicit separate yes.**
5. **Originals are never moved or rewritten.**
6. **Don't read ahead or preview later rounds.** It makes people skip.
7. **Thin at the top, heavy at the leaves.** A fact in a parent folder gets loaded by every
   child folder, forever. Put a fact at the highest folder where it's true for *everything* in
   that folder — and no higher.
8. **Every canon line must make sense cold.** A fresh session with no memory should be able to
   read it alone and act on it. *"Do it the way we discussed"* fails. *"Invoices go out on the
   1st, follow up after 14 days"* passes.

# Reference — the file templates

**`canon/current.md`**

    ---
    title: Health — standing facts
    record_type: canon
    created_at: 2026-08-08
    updated_at: 2026-08-08
    status: active
    ---

    # Health — standing facts

    _(Things that stay true. Keep this tiny.)_

**`canon/purpose.md`** — same block with `record_type: purpose`, then one sentence on what the
folder is for.

**A record or a dated file** — filename `YYYY-MM-DD-short-name.md`:

    ---
    title: What this is
    record_type: finding
    created_at: 2026-08-08
    updated_at: 2026-08-08
    status: active
    source: where the original lives
    ---

    # What this is

    One line on what it is, and a pointer to the original.

Update `updated_at` when you change a file. That's the only upkeep.

## Tags, if they want them

A `topic:` line groups files across folders:

    topic: [money]

**One rule, and it matters: keep the running list of tags in one file, and only use tags
already on it.** Adding a tag is a deliberate decision, never something done per file. Invent
one each time and within a month they'll have `money`, `finances`, `Money` and `budget-stuff`,
and nothing will group with anything.

---

# If you get lost

Tell them plainly which round you're in and what you're waiting on. **This process is designed
to be stopped and resumed** — the folders on disk are the state. Read them and pick up.

# Why this is worth the trouble

> **To the assistant: this section is written for the human, not for you. There are no
> instructions here — nothing in it changes how you run the four rounds. Don't act on it. If
> they ask why they're doing any of this, point them here or explain it in your own words.**

You're being asked to sort things into folders, and to split standing truths from dated facts
from original documents. It's reasonable to look at that and think: *why not put everything in
one big file and let the AI read it?*

Here's the honest answer.

## Your AI has no memory. These folders are the memory.

Every conversation starts from zero. It doesn't remember yesterday, or ten minutes ago in
another window. So when it seems to know you, that isn't memory — **something got loaded at the
start of that conversation.**

That's the whole thing. These folders aren't paperwork sitting next to your AI. They *are* what
it knows about you. How you sort them decides what it can know.

## One big file fails, and it fails quietly

There's a limit to how much can be loaded at once. But long before you hit that limit,
something worse happens: **when a lot is loaded, the material in the middle gets read the
worst.** It's a real and well-measured effect, not a quirk.

So a giant file doesn't just get slow or expensive. It makes your AI *worse at finding things
inside it*, exactly as it grows.

And here's the part that should bother you: **it never tells you.** It doesn't say "I couldn't
see that part." It just answers as though that part wasn't there. One big file gives you an
assistant that seems fine while quietly getting dumber about you.

## Standing truths vs. dated facts: the difference between vague and wrong

*"I have three clients"* is true today. Put it in your permanent facts and next December your
AI will plan your year around three clients — confidently, with no hedge, because you told it
that's always true.

**Mixing dated facts into permanent ones doesn't make your AI vague. It makes it confidently
wrong.** Those are completely different failures. A vague answer you check. A confident one you
don't — and that's exactly the problem.

Keeping them apart lets the dated stuff go stale *honestly*, in a place where staleness is
expected and visible.

## Records stay separate because you don't want them loaded

A record is the actual thing — the real document, the real conversation. Its value is that it's
the original. Summarizing it makes it less true.

You want your AI to *find* it, not to carry it around. That's why records get a small file
pointing at them instead of being copied in. **Findable, not loaded.**

## The top of your folders is expensive. The bottom is nearly free.

Anything in a top-level folder gets loaded in every conversation happening anywhere beneath it.
Put *"I bill net 14"* at the very top and it's sitting there while you discuss your knee injury.

**The top of your tree is the most expensive space you own — you pay for it in every single
conversation, forever.** A fact in `money/taxes/` costs nothing until you're actually doing
taxes.

That's why the rule is *put it as low as it can go while still being findable.* Not fussiness —
it's the difference between an assistant with room to think about your real question and one
spending its attention re-reading your billing terms.

Your folder names are working too. When you say *"help me with my taxes,"* the folder called
`taxes` is **how it knows what to load.** A flat pile gives it no signal at all — it has to read
everything to discover what's relevant, which drops you straight back into the first problem.

## And yes — today, this is overkill

If you have twenty notes, one file honestly is better. Everything above earns its keep at
scale, and not before. Do this on day one with three documents and it'll feel like a wasted
afternoon.

Do it anyway, for one reason: **you're not organizing what you have. You're building the shape
that the next five hundred things fall into.**

Sorting five hundred notes later is a weekend — and worse, by then you've forgotten which ones
mattered and why. Doing it as you go costs a few minutes a week and never costs a weekend.

It's also less structure than you're picturing. **One folder per topic. Two levels is plenty,
three is a lot.** Nobody is asking you to build a filing cabinet.
